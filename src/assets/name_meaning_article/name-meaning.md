My Name Is Not an Inconvenience

O-gay-chi. Oh-gey- she. Oh-geeck-chi. Oh-goy-she. I have had my name butchered my entire life. For years, I let it slide. I didn't want to make things awkward in my classrooms or harder on my teachers and friends. Plus, I figured that it didn't matter anyway. I told myself that the work at hand was more important than how my name was said. Unfortunately, it did start to impact me. I cringe every time my name was mispronounced during class attendance. I wondered if my ideas and thoughts were ever valued if people couldn't even try to pronounce my name in class. I agonized over how I was going to correct an entire school of people that had been saying my name incorrectly for the past four years. Growing up outside of Nigeria, it became easy to internalize this different pronunciation of my name as one that was comfortable and familiar. However, my parents wouldn't know whose child the teacher was calling if they heard my Americanized name. At one point, hearing my name in class created a sense of shame and dissociation from my culture. While it made me feel excluded and awkward, it also spurred me to investigate the history and meaning behind my name.

September 2001. Two days after the terrorist attack on the World Trade Center. My parents went to the doctor's office for the twelve-week scan. Instead of coming out of that room with the joyful news of how their baby was developing, my mother was told that she had a silent miscarriage, meaning the baby died long ago, but her body hadn't shown any signs. I will never be able to fully imagine how devastated my parents were. Whenever she shares this story with my sister and me, I can see the emotion and devastation in her face. I never realized that miscarriages were a long process of pain and heavy bleeding. The reality is that fertility and miscarriages are covered with shame and silence. One in four women will gain membership to this secret club that no one wants to join. However there continues to be pervasive taboos around the subject. Professor Zeynep Gurtin of women’s health at the University College of London states this “is partly because around 85% of miscarriages occur within the first trimester, before most women publicly announce their pregnancies (Gurtin 1).” It seems like anything that has to do with a woman's physical health is a taboo topic. We are not given the right to express our feelings and pain. After telling her that the miscarriage was due to inadequate rest and the stress of working three jobs, the doctor just sent my mom home and with no advice about what to do.

Two years later, my parents were moments away from finally becoming parents. My mother had been carrying my brother Justin for almost nine months. There was baby proofing stuff around my grandmother's basement in Canada. Then SARS spread globally, locking down everything in Canada. With the outbreak mostly confined to hospitals, the doctors advised my mother only to come in when her water broke. Three days passed after her scheduled date of labor, and she still didn't feel her water break. So against the doctor's and national expert's advice, she rushed to the hospital, wanting to know what was going on. My big brother Justin was stillborn. She didn't realize that she was having contractions during those days in lockdown.

“Pregnancy is a period of increased vulnerability for migrant women,” stresses Frankie Fair, Dean of Nursing and Midwifery at Sheffield Hallam University. There is a consistent trend for poorer pregnancy outcomes amongst migrant women who are at greater risk of maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality when compared to native born women” (Fair 2) in western countries. A combination of discriminatory laws and policies leave affordable health care options limited for immigrants. Health care is a basic human right, and it is crucial that we ensure access to high quality, language-accessible, and culturally congruent health care services for all people, including immigrants, to ensure their health, well-being, and dignity. 

It is true that black women are strong. Everyday life throws punches at them and expects them to keep on moving, learning to cope with traumatic experiences. In many cultures, people see child loss as the mother's fault, that there is something wrong with a woman who has had recurring losses, or she may have been too eager. So the loss is seen as a punishment from God.

In West African culture, words have power, and names connect you to your identity. This is why names are valuable and chosen carefully. Names tell a story, and last names are the base of the narrative. So after years of misfortune and grief, on May 27, 2004, when my parents decided to name me Ogechi, I believed they did so to stamp a time when they were consoled, and when everything felt anew. Ogechi in Igbo means God's Time is Best. At that moment, God was willing and able to grant them a new life. Even though I can't speak the language or directly understand my family's experience in Nigeria, I feel proud to be connected to such a rich and thoughtful history. To be the joy of my parents.
