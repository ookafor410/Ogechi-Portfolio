The New Face of Work: Immigrants Fueling the Gig-Economy

By Ogechi Okafor

NEW YORK — In the rain and dropping cold temperatures, Joel Camillo straps his insulated plastic UberEats bag to his back and checks his cellphone for the fastest bicycle route before pedaling into the stream of cars honking and dodging pedestrians down the busy streets of Manhattan. One of 65,000 food delivery workers, many of them immigrants, constantly crisscrossing the five boroughs.

Time is money, and Mr. Camillo, the now 18-year-old “DREAMER” the only son of four sisters who migrated from the Dominican Republic at 10 years old, needs the cash despite the risks.

“I’m just a young guy out here trying to make it,” Camillo says, still fresh to delivery work starting in December 2022 simultaneously accepting orders on three apps, Instacart, UberEats, and Grubhub.

He works up to 10 hours most days, as long as the lithium battery keeping his bike powered can last. At the end of the week Camillo collects about $800, 30% of which he said is tips primarily, so roughly earning $8 per hour.

Camillo brings restaurant meals to customers as per orders from his ghost boss. He rushes to Thai food to a couple in the Upper East Side, then he receives a ping for another order delivering everything from food to groceries to toiletries. On the next order, the delivery app tells him to travel down Third Avenue, despite it being closed for repaving. When he finally reaches the location, a customer complains to him that their order for two sides of biscuits only contained one, something that is no fault of Camillo’s.

“It has mixed emotions. You just sit here, wait for an order, and then deliver it. And then again, and again, the same thing, but it's okay for now,” he said.

“In many ways, gig work is not new,” said Lindsey Cameron, assistant professor of management at The Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania. “The agricultural and domestic work our grandparents and great-grandparents did, could be considered the original gig work.”

“The use of a digital platform or the app linking workers and customers at the press of a button explains the heightened interest in this gigantic sector of the economy today.” 

According to Cameron, there are two main ways this differs from traditional employment. Firstly, these workers are independent contractors meaning they are self-employed and have the ability to set their own hours and move between different platforms easily. Second, algorithms replace human managers in the management system so they have the say in whether to hire, fire, reward, and discipline workers.

If he is late, Camillo risks a low rating from the customers, jeopardizing his ability to accept orders with large tips. The rhythm of his work is defined by an algorithm calculating where he will go and the most efficient use of his time, no matter the season, road closure, or holiday.

This ghost boss can “deactivate” Camillo at any time, without an explanation, removing him from the apps and pushing the young adult into unemployment. This non-human management system is a modern form of the employer-employee relationship, according to Cameron.

Most regular Americans are not willing to take these jobs, and our economy is at a level that it couldn’t function without people willing,” said Cameron.

“Either the pay is too low, the conditions are just unappealing or the work is valued as unsatisfactory and unskilled to the vast majority of native-born Americans.”

U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data from 2022 support Cameron’s assertion about the types of jobs that immigrants and native-born Americans are willing to do. Native-born Americans were much more likely to be employed in the management and professional sectors, while immigrants are 60 percent more likely to work in the service and transportation sectors.

“Immigrants are just trying to hustle out here,” said Camillo. “People come to the country for opportunity, even though they may use different ways in getting to their goals.”

The Workers Justice Project and Los Deliveristas Unidos, a joint collective advocating for the city’s delivery drivers, have been at the forefront of supporting the often-overlooked community. Helping to recover thousands in nonpayments from tech companies, the right to use the bathroom in restaurants, and supporting legal protection of workers' rights to basic benefits and fair hourly wages.

“Delivery apps have quickly become an integral part of the NYC restaurant ecosystem and drivers have contributed to the apps’ success. We strongly believe that workers deserve a minimum pay and deserve to live with dignity,” said Gustavo Ajche, Founder of Los Deliveristas Unidos.

The NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection data shows that app deliveries accounted for 15% of nearly $25 billion in restaurant sales from March 2021 to May 2022.

“The thousands of app-based food delivery workers around the city will finally get the pay increase they deserve, allowing them to better support themselves and their families after being denied a living wage for years.”

In June, the Mayor's Office adopted into law a minimum pay rate for app-based delivery workers in response to the lobbying campaigns by the Workers Justice Project and Los Deliveristas Unido. A minimum of $17.96 an hour for the time workers are active on each app or 50 cents for every minute they spend on a trip.

Food-delivery apps Uber Eats, Relay, Grubhub, and DoorDash immediately responded with lawsuits challenging the new rates from taking effect, which were struck down.

“This city has finally begun to properly value the delivery workers’ labor, their experiences, and risks,” said Ajche.

“We need to fight for more protections and remain vigilant to ensure companies do not undermine workers and exploit loopholes.”

Despite its problems, delivering for these apps has allowed Camillo to work for himself, a trait in a job he finds deeply satisfying compared to the structured nine to five he said. He’s able to focus on his education and saving up toward tuition at a college in the city while supporting his family.

“I'm just hoping that I can help my family. They've done a lot for me, and I don't want to be doing this for the rest of my life,” said Camillo. “One thing is that I will make them proud. I always believe in myself.”
