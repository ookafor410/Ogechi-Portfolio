Is the Pipeline Really Still Empty? Or is Big Tech Looking in The Wrong Place

By Ogechi Okafor

Prominent tech companies have made little progress in their goal of hiring more minorities, relying on traditional education standards despite countless funded initiatives flaming the interest of women and minority students, leaving Gen-Z questioning whether there’s a place for them in the workforce. 

Year after year tech diversity reports show low single-digit increases in the percentage of female, Black, and Hispanic employees. Google widened its workforce of color and women by only .4% in their new 2023 report, contrasting Microsoft which barely reported a .1% boost. 

“I think the “pipeline” argument is loosely smoke and mirrors. These companies don’t want to believe the problem is themselves and they’re still perpetuating this toxic culture,” said Don Tomaskovic-Devey, professor at the University of Massachusetts Amherst teaching the affairs that generate workplace inequality and solutions needed to achieve employment equity. 

“You can have a desegregated workplace without making it inclusive. But how can we make real progressive change?” 

Generation Z has begun to enter the workforce, with an influx of 20 million job seekers expected by 2025. But they face an unspoken competition amongst themselves: who has the most valuable education? 

Tech recruiters determine which new computer science graduate they’re going to court based on a tier list of prestige, according to Lauren Rivera, Professor of Management & Organizations at Northwestern University and author of Pedigree: How Elite Students Get Elite Jobs. 

Core schools, which employers draw the bulk of new hires, are elite institutions, campuses invested deeply in, companies fly current employees from across the country to host information sessions, receptions, and dinners, prepare candidates for interviews, and interview hundreds of prospective candidates every year. 

Target schools, usually state or public institutions, where firms host a handful of recruiting events, accept applications, and interview candidates, but on a smaller scale. Typically are bound to a lower quota for interviews given than core schools, before the start of recruiting season.

This leaves most students from non-listed schools, such as HBCUs, HSIs, Women’s colleges, and bootcamps, out of the game. Having to apply directly to the company's website and placed into a separate reviewing process. These applications are usually not considered as seriously as those of core and target candidates if they are considered at all. 

“Academic institutions are a brand that where you go says a lot about you. Brands are these identities, long-lasting, very hard to change things. How do we reduce the brand's influence in the case of universities in the job market?,” said Michael D. Smith, professor of Information Technology and Public Policy at Carnegie Mellon University and author of The Abundant University, his new book examining how technology can transform higher education. 

“You are buying a projector of information about the quality of the brand. The brand is a signal of information about the product, you the student.” 

Carnegie Mellon University is considered a “top feeder” school, with direct pipelines to premier companies within the technology sector, according to a College Transitions analysis of 70,000 entry-level engineering and information technology employees public data from LinkedIn across 12 of the most reputable American tech companies. 

Google employs 1,400 undergraduate CMU alumni more than any other Big Tech company, as of July 2022. Columbia University, Stanford University, and Massachusetts Institute of Technology came next in the ranking producing more than 600 graduates each for Google. 

“We should be ashamed of ourselves. We are desperately trying to protect the status quo and the research tells us that the status quo sucks. These metrics reflect our values as educators. We need to completely overhaul the flawed system,” said Smith. 

In 2014, tech companies acknowledged the gender and education gap and made it a public goal to increase diversity in their workforces. 

The federal government under the Obama Administration allocated $1.4 billion to the National Science Foundation to accelerate STEM education and workforce development, helping to fund scholarships, invest in technical infrastructure, and develop curriculum and career support programs in “non-listed” universities. 

This year, Big Tech – Microsoft, Google, and Meta – has pledged millions of dollars to organizations and initiatives dedicated to addressing educational disparities preventing underserved youth from entering and thriving in STEM careers. 

“These early career programs help to demystify the workforce for these girls and give them the confidence to succeed even within this “All Boys Club,” said Poonam Mantha, Senior Manager, Communications and Public Relations at Girls Who Code, the world’s largest nonprofit organization committed to closing the gender gap in new entry-level tech jobs by 2030.

“How can we take that spark and turn it into a big ball of fire? When girls enter CS degrees it’s hard for these departments to retain them. With our College Loop programs, we help girls maintain their fire and persistence. It’s about helping girls who are in CS make it all the way through with technical interview prep and community.” 

GWC has inspired, educated, and equipped 580,000 girls, women, and nonbinary individuals with computing skills beginning in 2012 as part of a White House Science & Technology initiative. 

“For me, I didn’t have the easiest experience in college. My parents were not from America and they couldn’t really prepare me for the college or career world. Those early adult years can be so difficult and confusing so going into it at least knowing what to expect is a valuable leg up,” said Mantha. 

A study by Accenture and Girls Who Code finds the industry’s “bro-culture” reputation has continued to deter diversity in the field. Their story showed that 50% of women abandon technology careers by the age of 35 and are leaving tech roles at a 45% higher rate than men. 

“It’s not enough to hire, we know that. Getting the job is not easy and it’s difficult to retain the job,” said Mantha. 

Girls Who Code has strategic relationships with companies such as Accenture, Bank of America, Morgan Stanely, Apple, and AT&T donating over a million dollars to their programming. 

“One of our partner companies hired an HR person to work specifically with GWC hires to recreate a community that GWC provides but in the workplace. Their own chat room, their own representatives, their own events to build a culture that nurtures and mentors these girls,” said Mantha. 

Only 21% of women in the study said they believed the technology industry was a place they could thrive, which falls precipitously to 8% for women of color. 

“Tech culture doesn’t exist in a vacuum. We are trying to build a culture that is welcoming and accepting of all identities and experiences in the workforce,” said Mantha. 

“That’s a big part of the commitment and the high expectations we have with partners. They’re accountable for the way they treat their employees, and our former students, they bring into the workforce. Somebody has to break the standard.” 

Organizations such as Girls Who Code have made considerable progress in gender and racial representation in technical roles with 50% of their alumni choosing to major in Computer Science at a rate 7 times the national average and serving 50% of girls from historically underrepresented groups. 

However, the talent “pipeline” may not be as empty as some believe but instead, a locked old door preventing many skilled and proficient college graduates from ever stepping foot in the interview room.

“We need a pathway for people with non-traditional or elite education backgrounds to signal their skills to employers. We haven’t solved this and that’s one of the reasons stalling diversity in the workforce,” said Smith. 

“The employers need to tilt the scale to achieve diversity. Once we flatten that curve we can solve an algorithmic problem.”
