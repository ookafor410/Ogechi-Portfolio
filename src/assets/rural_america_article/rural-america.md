
Do Not Forget About Rural America:

Reversing the Decline of Rural Communities through Smart Technology

By: Ogechi Okafor

Welcome to Small Town, USA! No hustle and bustle. No long lines of traffic on the freeway. The fresh air, lots of green space, plenty of parks to enjoy the peace and quiet. The beautiful town looks like it was pulled straight out of a classic Hallmark Christmas movie. What's not to like about the countryside? Nonetheless, even with all this charm, rural areas struggle to sustain themselves in many parts of the country. 

Historically, rural areas have been critical to the social and economic well-being of America. The US Census Bureau estimates that 60 million Americans, or roughly one in every five, live in rural areas. Unfortunately, many of these communities have been overlooked and excluded by forces such as globalization and digitalization in the 21st century. 

Digital connectivity has become essential for economic and social activity. Unfortunately, nearly a third of rural residents lack broadband access than just 2 percent of city residents who lack access to only high-speed fixed broadband services. Even when broadband connectivity is available, coverage maps can be misleading. While they may show that some rural residents have access to broadband internet, the service may be slow, unreliable, or expensive. 

In the era of the coronavirus, millions of Americans are still working remotely. But if you live in a rural town, the pandemic has made you even more isolated than ever. Previously, people would often park outside the local library after hours to catch the Wi-Fi signal. Now that many public spaces that people relied on for the internet have closed, an employee that isn't considered essential can't work online, so they lose their income. “Students without the internet cannot join their classmates on Zoom, setting them back in the school year. Workouts are going online. Therapy is going online. Online shopping has been deemed the safest option. (Fugett, 2020)” So while we were bingeing the latest Netflix show and hosting virtual Zoom parties, many rural Americans were essentially living in a pre-internet society. 

There's a vicious cycle happening in most rural communities. Young people are packing up their bags and venturing out to the big city in search of better opportunities that can't be found in the declining agricultural and manufacturing sector. The typical rural county can lose up to 30 percent of its high school class within a couple of years of graduation. Leaving behind an older population, a smaller workforce, and a diminished tax base. Causing rural communities to actually have a much higher rate of concentrated poverty — tracking counties where more than a fifth of the population is poor — 31% concentrated poverty rate compared to 19% for cities. Meaning more people continue to leave, and as a result, businesses part from the community. The cycle simply repeats until entire towns around the country become modern ghost towns.

Of all the terrible things that resulted from the pandemic, the one good thing it has given is the rare chance to makeover rural America. To convince people they can have a future away from the congested metropolitan areas. This can be observed in real-time, as the country has seen the reverse migration of people escaping the city to rural communities due to Covid-19. Validating the critical need for smart rural towns and communities to create new sources of value, produce new ways of working, and improve productivity in key industries. If no action is taken, this "won't be sustained after COVID. People will eventually go back to the city life and forget about these small towns unless there are programs and infrastructure that can be developed to build more connectivity between these areas," says Mr. Remington Tonar, an innovation consultant for Fortune 500s, startups, as well as local, state, and federal government organizations. He is the Co-founder and COO of Cart.com, an eCommerce infrastructure company. 

So now you're probably wondering, how exactly can these problems be solved? The answer is to extend the potential of the Smart City model from urban to rural. Allowing us to create not just Smart Cities but interconnected Smart Regions, wherever they happen to be? Utilizing real-time technology, like the Internet of Things (IoT) devices and 5G networks, to collect data. Then implementing the results found to provide services and solve community problems, such as improving transportation and accessibility, improving social services, promoting sustainability, and giving its citizens a voice. 

Connectivity is the ultimate goal that smart regions need to work towards. Yes, that does mean “expanding affordable broadband coverage to rural areas around America, but it also means physical connectivity. (Tonar, 2021)” The internet moves around information, and infrastructure helps to expand opportunities and trade goods. The challenging part is that undertaking colossal infrastructure projects is extremely expensive. It's costly to lay broadband, and it's even more costly to build, like a high-speed rail from point A to point B. The high expense may hinder people from thinking about investing. However, by thinking regionally, the project will benefit the area. 

It looks like progress is happening right now. The Biden Administration has announced its support of the new Bipartisan Infrastructure Framework. The plan seeks to “connect every American to reliable high-speed internet while driving down prices for internet service to closing the digital divide by investing $65 billion. (United States Gov., 2021)” Also, the desire to invest $49 billion in sustainable transportation by modernizing and expanding transit and rail networks across the country. 

Far from being a lost cause, rural communities still have a tremendous amount to offer America, from affordable land to family-friendly neighborhoods to untapped knowledge and know-how. Making these resources accessible while connecting rural areas to the benefits of cities is economically wise and necessary for restoring a sense of national unity. Achieving this goal will require rethinking the notion of smart cities to include rural communities as well.
