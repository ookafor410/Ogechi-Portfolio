New York City Is Going to Flood. Faith Leaders Are Ready to Act as First Responders. 

By Ogechi Okafor

On a gray November morning in Lower Manhattan, Apostle David T. Cockfield stood in front of a folding table covered in brochures about flood insurance, cooling centers, and disaster response. Clergy, organizers, and government officials shuffled around him inside the conference room of the “Building Hope & Safety in a Changing Climate” forum. He wasn’t there as a distant observer. He had lived everything the speakers were warning about. 

“Three feet of water,” he said softly, remembering the most recent autumn storm that inundated Far Rockaway. “The whole roof [of the church’s newly opened community center] caved in and we had water in the basement even come up the steps. The walls and the floors, damned with mold.” 

At Battalion Pentecostal Assembly Church in Arverne, flooding is routine. Hurricane Sandy wrecked the community in 2012. COVID hit it even harder, “over 800 deaths,” Cockfield recalled, “four tractor trailers of bodies at the hospital.” 

Then, this fall, another flash flood filled the church again. “When disaster hits, we’re the first ones people in the community call and we don’t say no.” 

In the conference room surrounded by planners and policymakers, Cockfield told a story about obligation as he led the room in prayer. “We blanket the community with all different sources of help,” he said. “Food pantry, clothing giveaway, youth programs, disaster response, we try to help those families. We get to people before the Red Cross does.”

Climate disasters are no longer rare occurrences in New York City but now rhythmic events. Nearly 900,000 residents face high flood risk according to The Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s 2024 community development report finding roughly 400,000 at-risk households reside in low to moderate-income neighborhoods. As the water rises, faith leaders across the five boroughs are filling the gap between what government systems’ promise vs deliver, stepping into a climate adaptation role the city has never formally assigned them. They are the ones residents call when basements flood, when the power fails during heat waves, when families need help understanding evacuation orders or navigating FEMA. Faith leaders aren’t just responding to present disasters, they're preparing for the next ones and building long-term neighborhood resilience from the ground up. 

Before the forum’s first panel even began, Lucy Cummings, the Faith Sector Resilience Director at New York Disaster Interfaith Services (NYDIS), articulated the central contradiction of the work.

“Faith leaders are incredibly busy people with a lot of competing demands on their time,” she said. “So they often won't have the bandwidth to be involved in preparedness during blue skies.” But when disaster hits, they will “step up when their own people, their own community is impacted.” 

It’s an impossible position: expected to act like emergency managers but without the funding, staffing, communications tools, or training government agencies rely on. A preparedness gap, Cummings said, leads to predictable consequences: Power struggles. Mixed messages. Repeating mistakes. 

“It can become the disaster after the disaster,” she warned, sharing the importance of “connecting with people who’ve done this before” to learn about the resources that exist. “The time demands are real, but when we work in coalition during blue skies, our community benefits and we’re able to help people more quickly and get those resources out in a way that can save people's lives.” 

NYDIS formed the Faith Sector Climate Adaptation Working Group to close the gap in partnership with Rebuild by Design. A non-profit organization to raise the bar for response, preparedness, and resilience, The initiative arose during President Obama’s Hurricane Sandy Rebuilding Task Force and the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD) in response to the $19 billion in damages and over 69,000 destroyed housing units caused by the 2012 storm.

According to HUD’s 2013 Hurricane Sandy Rebuilding Strategy report, the storm caused more than $19 billion in damage in New York City alone and damaged or destroyed over 69,000 housing units. 

During the forum, the cohort of 22 interfaith leaders representing all five boroughs, released the “Faith Ready: Climate Adaptation Toolkit.” A digital information center which Cockfield now utilizes in community alert meetings with his congregation; offering templates for communication plans, flood-risk messaging, extreme-heat strategies, and guides for coordinating with the government. 

Over the last 6 months, the working group has collaborated alongside climate specialists, to build their capacity to communicate with their constituencies about climate hazards, disaster preparedness, and long-term climate adaptation planning. 

“Our whole intention was not just to talk about these things, but it’s about putting together a toolkit... so that we have information that would help.” 

Still on the ground, pastors and imams say the climate is changing faster than the systems meant to support them. 

A resident’s home purchased today would likely flood at least once before the end of their 30 year mortgage payment. As a 2023 analysis from Rebuild by Design, found across the five boroughs, over $3.6 billion worth of one-to-three family homes sold were located in areas projected to flood. That represents 7,300 homes, roughly one out of every five such homes sold in New York City.

Neighborhoods Under Water 

With limited choices of the building which one calls home, faith institutions are often more resilient than the physical infrastructure around them. In places facing both housing instability and climate threat, social infrastructure, the networks of trust, familiarity, and local connection becomes a lifeline. 

Layered edge-to-edge along the walls of Imam Izak-EL Mu’eed Pasha’s office at the Masjid Malcolm Shabazz were framed photographs of Malcolm X at rallies, longtime congregants shaking hands with city officials, community leaders gathered shoulder to shoulder in decades-old snapshots. Each image marked a moment in the mosque’s long civic life and the room reflected a space where faith, politics, and neighborhood advocacy in Harlem have long intersected. 

“People didn’t even know they were being priced out,” he said, recalling the early days of organizing against displacement. “Now they know because they’re living it.” 

In Harlem, stormwater flooding is rising and in a community where renters far outnumber homeowners, resilience and advocating against displacement depends on having trusted, local messengers. 

NYC’s Flood Hazard Mapper shows over 54% of all neighborhood properties in East Harlem risk flooding. A total of 1,654 properties, a dramatic rise from the 24 properties affected in 2012. 

“We need to stay engaged,” relayed Pasha. That’s where faith leadership plays a strong part, because we get the ear of the people even on a Sunday, Saturday, or Friday to make them stay involved.” 

The mosque created its own 501(c)(3) development corporation in 1994 to build community-first projects such as affordable housing suited to neighborhood needs. “That’s our religion,” Pasha said plainly. “We provide for our constituency and the whole community, not just Muslims but whoever's in need.” 

New York City is currently implementing several major climate-infrastructure projects, including the $1.45 billion “East Side Coastal Resiliency (ESCR)” project. One of the largest urban flood-protection systems in the country, which will create 2.4 miles of floodwalls, elevated parkland, and storm surge barriers along Manhattan’s east side. 

Beyond the seawalls and pumps, when storms come, faith institutions stay open even when government offices close. 

“Here, we don’t ask who you are or where you’re from,” Pasha said. “We were here for longevity, part of the community.” 

During the forum, advocacy organizer Vrinda Jagota at Hindus for Human Rights spoke about a problem familiar to anyone who works with immigrant or undocumented communities: risk messaging can sometimes create more fear rather than more safety. 

In a previous organizing effort unrelated to climate, she noticed turnout was low because the messaging unintentionally signaled danger. “It reminded us that a good organizer listens more than they speak,” she said. “You have to understand what people perceive as risk.”

People won’t evacuate, seek cooling centers, or apply for flood assistance if they don’t trust the messenger. 

Jagota’s group applies this principle to its climate-oriented activities, such as their seva-centered beach cleanups that draw over 150 volunteers. “We talk about caring for the earth as community care,” she said. “Preparedness resonates when it feels like part of who you are, not something imposed.” 

Climate vulnerability is not only about the storms and disasters that cause physical damage, describes Jagota. Instead it’s about “who feels safe enough to seek help.” Faith leaders' cultural fluency and deep trust networks allow them to touch communities government messaging fails to reach. 

The role of trust became even clearer in the stories shared by Rev. Karen Pershing, co-chair of the Staten Island Inter-religious Leadership Coalition. 

Storm surges reached up to 14 feet along the coastal blocks of Staten Island during Superstorm Sandy. 24 people died and more than $1 billion in property losses occurred , according to the NYC OEM Sandy After-Action Report, but faith institutions didn’t wait for instructions and quickly acted to aid the vulnerable. 

Pershing describes how creating one central organizing space allowed them to coordinate skilled volunteer groups, rebuild queues, and provide faster support to impacted residents; actions that implicitly saved time and improved recovery. 

“We could not have invited in National VOAD rebuild organizations unless Staten Island houses of worship had been willing to provide them with a place to sleep,” Pershing said, recounting how one church in the community opened its fellowship hall for months during the time of need. 

“It’s normally an important source of income for them. But when our community was hit, they told us, ‘Take the space for free for your weekly meetings.’ That room became our hub, where residents, rebuild groups, nurses, attorneys, and nonprofits came together. It was the space we needed to build relationships and organize.” 

Pershing’s experience coordinating Staten Island’s recovery has shaped her conviction that faith leaders are indispensable communicators during climate emergencies, especially for people in basement apartments, senior buildings without AC, or immigrant households who may distrust government notices. 

“I’ve seen success when faith leaders own their special role as trusted messengers,” she said. “The city could pour advertising money into subway ads about registering for Notify NYC, but if an entire community of faith leaders said, ‘Okay, this week we’re all going to encourage our congregants to sign up, and show them how to do it in their language of preference,’ that’s going to go a lot further.” 

When asked how she navigates power struggles among government agencies and clergy during disasters, Pershing didn’t hesitate. 

“Sometimes the folks who really want to take charge and be the spokesperson for the community are not always the most helpful. The most helpful people are willing to do the dirty work of making the email blasts and designing the resource guide, that's often unpaid, unrecognized, but it is what goes the furthest in actually helping one's community to recover.” 

Building A Network 

Climate resilience and response is not heroic improvisation. It is coordinated, relational work. 

“Lift up the servant leaders. The ones doing the hard work,” she echoed. “Everyone's voice is welcome at the table, but strengthening communities together excels through a recovery coalition plan. If you have a written communications plan, then you already know who is going to play which role and goes a long way in preventing power struggles in the community from happening in the first place.” 

In parts of the city, climate pressure never lets up and becomes a compounding nature of visible disasters. Nowhere is this clearer than in the Rockaways, where 97% of properties have risk of flooding, far exceeding the 22% city wide average, according to the First Street Foundation’s 2024 National Risk Assessment. 

Storms routinely push water across the peninsula bordered by the Atlantic on one side and Jamaica Bay on the other, deepening risk for the low-lying and densely populated area. 

“Rockaway is a food desert and a health desert,” Cockfield said. “We have one hospital for around 135,000 people.” 

During Sandy, his church became a de facto aid hub. “We housed about 15 different organizations in our church building. We lost everything,” said Cockfield. Government organizations weren’t able to get to us and we were the only church in the middle of a peninsula 11 miles long.” 

Through COVID, it became a vaccination site, spreading urgent health information to black and brown residences. “We had to be the face for the community, telling them it's okay,” voiced Cockfield. “The community trusts us, so because of that, they were able to come and receive help.” 

Battalion Pentecostal Assembly works closely with the Community Intergenerational Action Network (CIAN), a 501(c)(3) organization, to support the community in disaster response and preparedness, as well as through the church’s own services, including a pantry that feeds around 450 people biweekly. 

Pastor Cockfield described CIAN as a year-round engine for climate and resource education, continually bringing knowledge back into the neighborhood and practical skills for residents.

Just after the 13th anniversary of Sandy, it became a clean-up site again after heavy flooding on October 30. “The one hospital [St. Johns Hospital] can’t handle everything,” Cockfield asserted. Pastor Cockfield serves on a task force for Councilwomen Selvena N. Brooks-Powers with a goal of creating a community trauma center. Councilwoman Brooks-Powers represents the 31st City Council District and is Majority Whip of the City Council as well as Chair of its Committee on Transportation and Infrastructure. 

“We’re still trying to get ourselves back together,” Cockfield said. “Many homes are still zombie homes. People walk away from their homes. Others died.” And now, gentrification. 

Across Beach Sixth Street, gleaming towers of new developments highrises replacing bungalows swept away after Sandy. “If you came to Rockaway 10 years ago and you come back now, it would be different. Rockaway is having gentrification just like Harlem did,” said Cockfield. Many people who stayed and kept the community now cannot even live here because of how the cost of everything has skyrocked.” 

The pressures unfolding in the Rockaways are not isolated. They mirror places already strained by inequity further absorbing the brunt of climate change. In uptown Manhattan, Harlem faith institutions that have served as cultural and civic anchors for generations are confronting a parallel crisis: environmental displacement and cultural erasure. 

“In Harlem, preservation is not just buildings. It’s culture, our sense of place,” said Claudette Brady, Executive Director of Save Harlem Now, a non-profit advocacy organization dedicated to protecting the neighborhood’s heritage. “The people who worked with you on the block associations, on our tenant associations, what did they get? They got displaced. It's almost like being punished for your good work, and it's your children and grandchildren who can't be here. That's not what we fought for. That's not what we were working for.” 

Beatrice Sibblies, Managing Partner of BOS Development and Faculty Lecture of Real Estate Development and Construction at Columbia University, laid out the stark financial realities affecting residents. “The people in my community cannot afford to pay $5,000 a month in rent. Even at $52,000 - 53,000 a year, $36,000 of that going to rent leaves you how much to live on? It’s not a quality of life.” 

In spring of 2015, Mayor de Blasio signed Local Law 18, which called for the creation of a Hurricane Sandy Charitable Organizations and Houses of Worship Recovery Task Force to better understand the critical role faith organizations played in Hurricane Sandy recovery efforts. The fifteen appointed religious leaders conducted a post-Sandy analysis reporting “over 200 New York City houses of worship of many different faith traditions, coordinated rebuilding efforts and served as relief sites providing essential items such as food, toiletries and water.” 

Clergy are knitting together a citywide, community-led resilience infrastructure. Faith leaders are increasingly turning toward one another to trade strategies, share resources, and collectively prepare for the next climate event ages before it strikes. 

During the forum, founder and board member of South Bronx Unite, Mychal Johnson, described how after Sandy brought devastating flooding to the peninsula and received “very little attention” from officials. “If we don’t prepare ourselves for the next one, no one else will.” 

With the leadership of Johnson, the members of the South Bronx community developed their own joint flood-mitigation and resiliency plan for the 60,000 residents. “Ground up decisions. Ground up plans. Ground up self determination,” he said. “People are experts of their own community.” 

Bronx County faces an extreme flood risk over the next 30 years, according to the First Street Foundation. 

“We haven’t been listened to,” he said. “So we have to communicate in a way everyone understands, not jargon but the true things that affect our children and families.” 

One congregation’s lessons become another’s early warning. What began as scattered local efforts is quickly evolving into a coordinated faith-based climate network. This philosophy of starting with the community and starting with the truth is embedded in the Faith Sector Climate Adaptation Working Group, which Cockfield, Pershing, Jagota, Johnson, and a dozen others participate in. 

Their toolkit is a compilation of actionable ways to message guidance when communicating about climate resilience through the bi-weekly meet the working group had to train leaders on communicating extreme weather alerts, helping immigrants navigate FEMA, mapping local flood risks, and advocating for green infrastructure, and more.

Even with coordination, the demands are overwhelming. Long before the most recent storm, Cockfield had watched the city delay or divert the investments his neighborhood needed.

“He [former Mayor de Blasio] literally set aside...I think it was $150 million to start doing the infrastructure,” Cockfield recalled. “And the night an organization was honoring me, Donovan Richards [former 31st district Councilman] came to me and he said, ‘Pastor, I got some good news and I got some bad news.’ The good news was they were paving the streets in Rockaway. The bad news was the mayor moved the money from what he had planned for the infrastructure and gave it to someone else... Maybe we don’t have enough powerful people who can advocate on our behalf. There’s only so much I can do.” 

By the time the recent October storm hit, the consequences were predictable. The bay surged so high that even elevated roads flooded. Drains backed up. The church took water yet again. 

“They need to build bulkheads,” he said. “This is infrastructure failure.” 

But faith leaders aren’t engineers. They aren’t emergency managers. They aren’t climate scientists. They don’t have the budgets to fix systemic problems. They can only respond. 

“People think we have money,” Cockfield said, laughing ruefully. “We don’t. A lot of this comes from personal contributions or insurance. If insurance even covers the damage.” 

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) calculates that an inch of floodwater can result in up to $25,000 in repairs, costs typically not covered by standard homeowners insurance. Internal FEMA documents indicate the agency is also less likely to grant some types of housing assistance to low-income and minority disaster survivors, according to an NPR investigation. 

For congregations, the funding landscape is even harder to navigate. 

“Sometimes you just got to cut your losses and go,” Cockfield explained. “If it’s a grant, you have to know how to write a grant. And most grant writers want their portion of the money upfront, while we have to wait with nothing and hope the reimbursement comes later.” 

Cummings echoed the concern. 

“Faith leaders can’t be expected to coordinate disasters alone,” she said. “But all the work they can do thanks to trust and presence, no city agency can replicate.” 

The danger is that the city will lean on that trust without supporting it. 

Across the city, from imams in Harlem to pastors in Staten Island, faith leaders are emerging not as symbolic figures, but as essential actors in the city’s survival. They aren’t waiting for the next disaster. This quiet revolution is reshaping the systems that determine who gets protected one congregation, one conversation, and one neighborhood at a time. 

Pastor Cockfield remains determined. Battalion Pentecostal Assembly plans to build an assisted living facility, a gym, even a school program with a hydroponic rooftop farm once they renovate the damaged community center.

Still, when asked whether he sees himself as a climate leader, Cockfield laughed. “I’m just God’s humble servant,” he said. “But if we don’t do it, who will?”
